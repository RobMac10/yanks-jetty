The story of Yanks Jetty

On the south-west side of Orpheus Island, also known as Goolboddi, in the sea country of the Manbarra people, a steel jetty reaches out over clear reef water. Locals call it Yanks Jetty.

During the Second World War, Townsville became a major supply base for Australian and American forces. Ships moving through these waters faced a hidden danger: magnetic mines, which could be set off by the magnetism of a steel hull. The answer was degaussing, passing electric current through cables to cancel a ship's magnetic field.

Queensland Parks and local histories say this jetty was a degaussing station for American ships and submarines, and that is how it got its name. But the story is not settled. The Naval Historical Society of Australia doubts it, noting that the United States Navy already had a degaussing range at the mouth of the Brisbane River, and that American submarines are not known to have used the Palm Islands. What is certain is that the Americans were here. In 1943, Navy Seabees built a Catalina flying boat base at nearby Palm Island, and a wartime water reservoir still stands behind the beach at Yanks Jetty.

Cyclone Yasi badly damaged the jetty in 2011. It was restored in 2013, and its pontoon was refurbished in 2015. Today, Hinchinbrook Shire Council looks after it, and visitors arrive by boat to snorkel, camp with a permit, and walk past wartime ruins to a lookout over Hinchinbrook Island.

When you visit, please respect this place, and the Manbarra people whose country it has always been.

A wartime jetty

By 1942 Townsville was a major supply base for the South-West Pacific war, used by Australian and United States forces. One of the threats to shipping was the magnetic mine, set off by the magnetic field of a steel hull. Degaussing reduced that field by passing electric current through cables around or near a ship, making it far less likely to trigger such mines.

Queensland Parks describes Yanks Jetty as a degaussing (demagnetising) station for American ships and submarines, and that is the source of its name. The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority lists it among the World War II support structures that remain in the Marine Park.

A contested story. The Naval Historical Society of Australia says the jetty is unlikely to have been used for degaussing, because the US Navy had a degaussing range at the entrance to the Brisbane River and US submarines are not known to have frequented the Palm Islands. No construction date, unit or list of ships that used the jetty has been confirmed.

The American presence nearby is well documented. In July 1943, Seabees of the 55th Naval Construction Battalion began building a US Navy Catalina flying boat base at Palm Island. It became operational on 25 October 1943 and closed in May 1944. At Yanks Jetty, a water reservoir built by US forces during the war has been recorded behind the beach, and a short walking track passes WWII ruins.

What's there now: the jetty seen today has been largely rebuilt. The 2013 works replaced the damaged walkway sections with aluminium gangways and built a new concrete abutment, while the existing piles were refurbished. The pontoon was refurbished in 2015.

Timeline

  • 1942: Townsville is a key Allied supply base.
  • c. 1942–45 (date unconfirmed): Jetty in use by US forces, traditionally as a degaussing station.
  • 1943–44: US Navy Catalina base at Palm Island (operational 25 Oct 1943, closed May 1944).
  • 1973: Orpheus Island becomes a national park.
  • 2011: Cyclone Yasi badly damages the jetty.
  • Jul–Aug 2013: Restoration under disaster-recovery (NDRRA) funding. Pacific Marine Group does the work for Hinchinbrook Shire Council, with AECOM managing.
  • 2015: Pontoon towed to Townsville for full refurbishment and then returned.

Visit today

Yanks Jetty is only reachable by boat. Orpheus Island is about 110 km north of Townsville and 45 km east of Ingham. The jetty is managed by Hinchinbrook Shire Council. The Yanks Jetty camping area is run by Queensland Parks, and you need a camping permit. The Yanks Jetty walking track (450 m return, about 20 minutes) starts 140 m east of the jetty and leads past WWII ruins to a view of Hinchinbrook Island. The jetty sits in the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, so check the zoning rules, protect the coral and take all your rubbish home.
